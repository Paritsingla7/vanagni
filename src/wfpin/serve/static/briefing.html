A briefing before the live demo

Vanagni

Tomorrow's forest-fire list for an Indian state: what it is, how it was built, what it found, and where it goes next.

vana = forest, agni = fire Uttarakhand and Odisha5 km cellsnext day backtested on held-out seasons

Open with the gap, not the model. One sentence: today's system shows what is burning, nobody shows what will burn.

01

What Vanagni is

Every evening, rank every 5 km patch of a state's forest by how likely a satellite is to see it burn tomorrow.

The gap

The Forest Survey of India's alert system (FAST 3.0) tells forest departments what is burning right now, from NASA satellite detections. There is no open, tested system that says what is likely to burn next.

The use

A forest division has a handful of patrol teams. A team can check about twenty places in a day. So the useful output is a short ranked list, and the score that matters is how many of the top 20 actually burn.

What it is not

Not a live service yet, and not a probability. It is a backtest: every past day was ranked by a model that had only seen earlier seasons, then checked against what the satellite saw.

Say it as a bet that could lose. That is what makes a "no" worth something.

02

The question it tests

Almost every forest fire in India is lit by people. So does knowing where people are (roads, villages, fields) predict tomorrow's fire better than the weather does?

90–95% of India's forest fires are reported as human-caused: litter burned before tendu and mahua collection, crop residue fires that escape into forest, grazing clearance. If that is the whole story, human-proximity features should beat weather. The claim was written down before any result, as something the evaluation could refute.

Lead with the ratio, then say it held in a second state and on places the model never saw.

What this does not mean

It does not mean people don't start the fires. It means where people are is already captured by fire history: places that are burned every year show up as history. What is still untested is when people burn (the collection and harvest calendar), because no district-level calendar exists to test it with.

The other finding

The operational Fire Weather Index on its own scores PR-AUC 0.038. The full model scores 0.190, about 5× better (Uttarakhand). The index rates how dry a region is; it cannot say which cell will burn.

05

What counts as a fire

A satellite "hotspot" is any hot pixel: crop-residue burning, brick kilns, factories, fires across the border. Five filters keep only forest fire:

  1. 397,346 raw detections (Uttarakhand, 2017–2026)
  2. one satellite only (Suomi-NPP), so detection density doesn't jump in 2018
  3. inside the state boundary, not the bounding box around it
  4. not a known industrial source; nominal or high confidence
  5. at least half the 375 m footprint is natural vegetation
  6. not a persistent hotspot (a spot that burns even outside the season)
  7. 104,440 qualifying forest-fire detections

Of the detections the vegetation filter removed, 94% sat on cropland. Without it, "near fields" would have looked like a great predictor.

The model is deliberately boring. The point is the comparison between feature families, so the learner is held fixed.

06

Why this model

XGBoost, gradient-boosted decision trees: one fixed configuration (400 trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.05) for every stage and every season.

Gradient-boosted trees

Built for tables of mixed features. Catches interactions ("dry and near a road") without hand-made terms, handles missing values, trains in minutes, and reports which features it leaned on.

Logistic regression

Too simple: risk is not a straight line in temperature or distance, and the interactions would need to be guessed in advance.

Deep learning

Needs far more fire days than exist, is hard to explain, and the study needs hundreds of fits (stages × seasons × spatial folds).

The baselines it must beat

Persistence ("burning today, burning tomorrow") and the operational Fire Weather Index. Beating nothing is not a result.

Fires are rare (about 1–3% of cell-days), so each training split re-weights positives (scale_pos_weight) from its own data. No per-stage tuning: any difference between stages comes from the features, not the knobs.

Four words carry this slide: ablation, rolling origin, spatial holdout, leakage. If asked "how do you know", it is one of these.

07

Why this process

Ablation: add one family at a time

Persistence → + fire history → + weather → + fuel & terrain → + human proximity. The lift at each step is what that family adds. Terrain and human features are split into separate steps so static "place" features can't take credit for people.

Rolling origin: never train on the future

To score a season, train only on the seasons before it, the way a real system would have to work.

Spatial holdout: places it never saw

Hide whole 50 km blocks from training and score them. A model that memorised "this cell burns" fails here; one that learned conditions survives.

Leakage tests: automated, on real data

A test rebuilds the data with fires cut off at a date and fails if any feature before that date changes. Static layers must never change by date.

Metrics that fit a rare event

PR-AUC: how well the ranking puts burning cells on top (random ≈ the burn rate, about 0.02). Precision@20: of the top 20 cells each day, how many burned. No accuracy: 98% "accuracy" is saying "no fire" every day.

Honest intervals

Days next to each other share weather, so intervals come from resampling whole weeks (7-day block bootstrap, 1,000 times), and lifts are compared on the same days.

Point at the two big steps (history, weather) and how flat the last one is. That flat step is the whole finding.

08

What each layer adds

Mean PR-AUC over the test seasons, each stage adding one family to the one above it.

Inside the full model, human-proximity features carry 3.5% of the model's gain in Uttarakhand; fire history carries 73.5%.

How it was developed

Written as a spec first (requirements, architecture, data sources, risks), then built phase by phase, each on its own branch, tested, then merged.

  1. Aug 2026Phase 0 Spec, scaffolding, and an audit of an earlier wildfire project to learn from its mistakes.
  2. Oct 2026Phases 1–2 Ingestion (FIRMS, ERA5-Land) and static layers; the cell × day panel and the label filter.
  3. Oct 2026Phases 3–4 Staged XGBoost models, the FWI baseline, rolling-origin evaluation, bootstrap, spatial holdout.
  4. Oct 2026Phases 5–6 The read-only API and demo screens; README and written report.
  5. Nov 2026Phase 7 A second state from config alone (Odisha); seasons that wrap the new year; the field-sheet redesign.
  6. Nov 2026Phase 8 Uttarakhand extended to six test seasons, 2021–2026.
  7. Nov 2026Phases 9–11 The demo as a static site on Vercel; Odisha's seasons; Odisha on the site.

What the checks caught

  • Rainfall ERA5 accumulates rain from midnight UTC; a plain difference silently lost an hour a day. Caught against a real month of data.
  • Fire Weather Index One constant was 10⁻⁴ instead of 10⁻⁶. Caught by the published reference case, which all outputs now match.
  • Wrong fires 38% of detections in the bounding box were outside the state, including crop-burning plains. Removed before they could flatter the hypothesis.
  • The map First version called scores "probabilities". They aren't calibrated, so the map now colours by rank.
Say these before you're asked. Owning the limits reads as rigour.

10

What it can't say

  • The label is a satellite detection, not a fire of known cause; cloud and gaps between passes hide some fires.
  • Weather (~9–11 km) is coarser than the 5 km grid: neighbouring cells share one sample.
  • Road distances use today's OpenStreetMap for every past year.
  • One state-wide fire calendar, so the "when people burn" half of the question is untested, not refuted.
  • Scores rank cells; they are not calibrated probabilities.
  • A handful of seasons per state; season-to-season swings are not inside the intervals.
End on the next step, concretely: score tomorrow from a real forecast and publish yesterday's hits every morning.

11

Where it stands, and what it wants to be

The goal: a daily list, for each forest division, of the places most likely to burn tomorrow, graded in public every day against what actually burned.

  1. Done

    The research result

    Which ingredients predict next-day fire: history and weather, not proximity. Two states, a backtest, the live demo. Odisha's further run is in progress.

  2. Next step

    Forward proof

    Score real tomorrows. Swap reanalysis weather (only known afterwards) for the free ECMWF forecast, pull FIRMS near-real-time fires, run every evening on a schedule, and publish a scorecard of yesterday's hits. Expect some loss of skill, and report it.

  3. Then

    A better model

    Test the timing half: tendu, mahua, harvest and jhum calendars by district. Add a real forest-type map (chir pine, sal), Indian reanalysis weather (IMDAA), and calibrated probabilities.

  4. The goal

    Real use

    A forest division using the list to place patrols, and the question that matters: are fires caught earlier?

The figures most likely to come up, in one place.
