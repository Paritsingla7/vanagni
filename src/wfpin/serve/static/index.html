The question

Almost every forest fire here is lit by people. So does knowing where people are, the roads, villages and fields, predict tomorrow's fires better than the weather does?

    What each layer adds

    PR-AUC, averaged over the four test seasons: how well a ranking puts the cells that burn at the top. Ranking at random would score about 0.02, the share of cells that burn on a given day.

    From hotspot to list

    1. Every hotspot from space

      342,805 heat detections over the state from NASA's VIIRS sensor, 2017 to 2024, each 375 m across.

    2. Keep only forest fire

      Farm burning, kilns and weak detections are removed by four filters. 91,268 detections remain.

    3. Cut the state into cells

      A 5 km grid: 1,950 cells with enough forest to burn, followed for 2,738 days.

    4. Add what was known that day

      Weather, recent fires, slope and fuel, roads and villages. Nothing from tomorrow: automated tests fail if any feature could see it.

    5. Rank tomorrow

      A gradient-boosted tree model, trained only on earlier seasons, orders every cell for the next day.

    6. Score against what burned

      Four seasons held out one at a time, intervals from resampled weeks, and a test on places the model never saw.

    Built from open data

    NASA FIRMS · Copernicus ERA5-Land · NASA SRTM · ESA WorldCover · OpenStreetMap · Forest Survey of India · Canadian Fire Weather Index

    Nothing here is a probability, an official warning or a claim about who lights fires. It is one state, four seasons and one research question.

    The finding is a negative one, and that is the useful part: the evaluation was built so that it could have come out the other way.
